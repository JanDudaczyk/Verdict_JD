How Verdict Debate works

From "they're speaking" to "point scored" — in under a second.

Say something true and the screen flashes green before you've finished your next breath. Say something false and it flashes red. Nobody's raising a hand to fact-check you — it's already done. Here's the machinery behind that, round by round.

1 Setup

Six formats, one screen. Pick Public Forum and the whole speech order snaps into place — constructives, crossfires, summaries, final focus, real time limits, zero guessing. Parliamentary runs the actual PMC → LOC → MG → MO → LOR → PMR sequence. Casual mode throws all of that out — you just set the rounds, the clock, and whether sides trade off.

Pick a topic — a preset or your own — name the debaters, set your spoken language, decide what points are worth, and choose whether an AI judge weighs in at the end.

2 Live speech capture

Hit start and the mic goes live. Words show up almost as fast as you say them — faded at first, while the recognizer's still deciding what it heard, then locked in solid once it's sure. That mic light isn't decoration; it's the only proof anyone's actually listening.

And while you're mid-sentence on your next point, the one you just finished is already getting checked behind the scenes.

3 Claim detection & fact-check

Every statement is sorted before anything happens:

  1. What kind of statement is it? A fact or statistic, an opinion, a value judgment, a prediction, a what-if, a claim about the case (Mock Trial), or something unverifiable. Views are logged with a label but never scored — you won't get dinged for saying "I think."
  2. Can it be settled right now? Well-known facts and statistics are judged on the spot. Harder ones — exact figures, recent events, niche sources — are held back and checked with a Wikipedia lookup the moment that person finishes speaking.

The checks are paced on purpose — sentences are grouped into one check every few seconds (longer when the AI service is busy), nothing already said is checked twice, and they go quiet during crossfire, because even we can't tell who's talking over whom.

4 Instant feedback

Facts that can be settled on the spot come back fast. Every statement gets a label:

  • True or mostly true — screen flashes green, a short high tone plays, and the speaker gets +fact (partial points for mostly true).
  • False or misleading — screen flashes red, a low tone plays, and the speaker loses −wrong (partial for misleading).
  • Opinion, value claim, prediction, hypothetical, case claim, unverifiable or inconclusive — logged in the claim log with the reason, and no points move.
  • To verify — held back until that person finishes speaking. If it turns out true or false, points are added or removed then and the review marks it "checked later."

After every turn, a short review appears: what the speaker argued, what worked, what was missing, whether they answered the other side, and one thing to try next. It's coaching only, and it never changes the score.

A banner surfaces the claim in plain English, one line of reasoning, and whoever just got scored. Every one of these calls is tagged · auto in the undo tray, in case the machine got it wrong and you need to say so.

5 Manual scoring & fallacies

The AI doesn't get the final word — you do. Missed a claim? Hit +fact or −wrong yourself. Heard a straw man or a slippery slope slide by? The −fallacy button pulls up all 16 — pick the one that fits and it docks the point and tags the transcript right where it happened.

Or skip the button entirely — highlight the phrase yourself in the live subtitles and a "Fact-check this" shortcut pops up, running the exact same check on demand.

6 End of debate

When the clock runs out — or you end it yourself — everything lands on Results: final scores, the fact / wrong / fallacy tally for each side, a highlight reel of every moment that got flagged, and the full transcript, ready to export.

Turn on AI Judging and you also get an actual verdict — a points-based one that just does the math, or a substance-based one that reads the whole transcript and calls who made the stronger argument.

Privacy

Speech is turned into text first. By default your browser does that itself — in Chrome and Edge it's done by Google or Microsoft, not by us. In high-accuracy mode, and for uploaded files, short audio clips are sent to our transcription service, and we don't keep them. For fact-checks, only a few recent sentences and the topic go to our AI service — no audio and no names — and claims about recent events are looked up on Wikipedia. Turn reviews send that turn's text, the opponent's turn before it, and the topic, also without names. The AI judge and analysis also send the round's transcript and the names you entered. Nothing about a round is saved on our servers unless you sign in, enter a league code, or play an online room. We also keep anonymous usage counts — which features get used and how long debates run — with no names, speech, or topics, and you can turn that off in the Privacy Policy. If the AI service is busy, checks slow down or pause and you can keep scoring by hand. The Privacy Policy has every detail.

In online debates, voice goes straight between the two debaters' browsers and we don't record it. Your mic is switched off except on your own turn.

In a ranked match, an AI judge reads the text of what both sides said (no audio) to pick a winner, and we save your rating and match history on your account.

When to overrule the AI

It will get things wrong sometimes — statistics, very recent events, and anything that hinges on exact wording are where it stumbles. It's tuned to stay quiet unless it's genuinely confident, but "genuinely confident" still isn't "certain." Watch the banner. If a call looks off, it probably is — undo is one click away in the tray, every time.

Verdict Debate is run by Jan Dudaczyk and Jeffrey Begun (New York metropolitan area, USA). This page describes what the app actually does with your data. We update it when the app changes; if anything here doesn't match what the app does, please tell us.

2. What happens to your speech and text, feature by feature

Live subtitles (default). Your browser's built-in speech recognition listens through the microphone you allowed. In Chrome and Edge that recognition is normally performed on servers run by Google or Microsoft, under their own privacy policies; the audio does not come to us. If your browser has no speech recognition, blocks it, or it stops returning words, the app automatically switches to the High-accuracy method below and tells you.

High-accuracy transcription, and Upload mode. The app records short clips from the microphone (about 3½ seconds each; longer if the service is busy; clips with no sound are skipped), or, in Upload mode, extracts the audio from the file you choose inside your browser and cuts it into chunks of up to about 2 minutes. Each clip or chunk is sent to our server, which forwards it to Groq's speech-to-text service and returns the text. Our server does not save the audio. Your browser may apply echo cancellation, noise suppression, and automatic gain to the microphone before capture; that happens on your device.

Live fact-checks, labels, and fallacy flags. The transcribed text is sent, through our server, to Groq: up to the 4 most recent sentences from the current speaker, up to 2 earlier sentences from the same speaker as context, and the debate topic. It does not include audio and does not include debater names. Groq returns a kind and verdict for each statement and any fallacies. If the main AI is busy, our server tries a backup AI from the same provider, and if that is busy too, the check is retried a little later.

Wikipedia lookups. For claims that look recent (for example they mention 2024 or later, or "recently" or "last year"), and for claims held back until the end of a speech, your browser sends the claim text directly to Wikipedia's search (run by the Wikimedia Foundation), which can see your IP address and that text. The first Wikipedia excerpts that come back are added to the request sent to Groq.

Turn reviews. When a turn ends (and the "Review every turn" option is on), the text of that turn, the opponent's previous turn if there was one, and the debate topic are sent through our server to Groq to produce coaching feedback. It does not include audio or debater names (the speaker is identified only by side, such as "Pro Team"). Reviews are kept in your browser's memory during the session, are shown on the results page, and are not saved anywhere when you leave. They never change the score. In Online Debate your own device does the same after each of your turns: it sends the text of your turn, your opponent's previous turn, and the topic for a review, and sends your held-back claims for verification. Your opponent's device does this for theirs, and neither of you can see the other's reviews.

Personal lessons. If you press "Make my lesson" on the Progress screen, the names and counts of your most frequent flagged fallacies (for example "Ad Hominem, 4 times") are sent through our server to Groq. Nothing else is sent: no speech, topics, or debater names. The lesson it writes is saved in your browser's local storage so it is still there next time, and you can replace it.

Which AI model. Live fact-checks use one Groq model; turn reviews, the AI judge, argument analysis, Case Checker, Case Builder, and personal lessons use a second Groq model, so coaching can't use up the live-check allowance. If one is at its limit, the other is used as a backup.

Online voice and live text. In Online Debate the two debaters hear each other over a direct, browser-to-browser audio link (WebRTC). The voice does not pass through our servers and we do not record it. To set the link up, our server relays two short connection messages between the debaters (they contain network addresses and are deleted when the debate ends, and otherwise after about 15 minutes, with the cleanup running whenever a new connection message arrives), and Google's public STUN servers help the two browsers find a route, which means Google and the other debater can see your IP address. Your microphone is switched off by the app except on your own turn (and during crossfire), and you can also mute yourself. The other debater's device is able to record what it receives, so only debate people you trust. While someone is speaking, their live transcript text goes straight to the opponent over the same link; if that link isn't available, the text goes through our server as a backup, replacing the previous text every second or so and deleted when the turn ends. Some strict school or office networks block the direct link; the app then says voice isn't available and the live transcript still works.

Ranked matches. When a ranked online debate ends, our server sends the debate topic and the text transcript of what each side said (words only: no audio, and no names or account details) to Groq, which acts as an AI judge and returns a winner (or a draw) and a short reason. The main Groq model is used, with the second one as a backup. The transcript text is what each debater's browser reported from speech recognition, so it can contain mistakes, and the judge can be wrong. Casual online matches are never sent to the judge. Ratings never use the score counters. The judge is also told how many facts, false claims and fallacies the automatic checks found for each side, as a minor hint, and returns a short 1-5 rating of each side's argument, evidence, rebuttal and clarity, which both players can see.

Study sets and Mock Trial tools. Flashcards, quizzes, matching games, and the objection and fallacy trainers run entirely in your browser; your progress is saved only in your browser's local storage. The Mock Trial case files are fictional practice material. The objection log is kept in your browser's memory for the round and shown on the results page; in Online Debate it is not shared.

Held-back claims. Claims that need a real lookup are kept in your browser's memory during the speech, verified when that person finishes speaking, and are not saved anywhere when you leave the page.

AI judge, argument analysis, Case Checker, Case Builder, Respect Filter (when you turn it on), and the manual fact-check box. The text you type, or the transcript of your round, plus the debater names and topic you entered where the feature uses them, is sent to Groq through our server. If in Setup you choose Anthropic or OpenAI and enter your own key for that account, those requests go from your browser straight to that company under its terms instead; your key is kept in your browser's memory for that session and is not saved by us.

What you can switch off. The microphone features only run after your browser's permission prompt. You can turn off Auto fact-check in Setup, leave the Respect Filter off (its default), and not enable High-accuracy transcription. If you do, text is sent only for the features you use.

3. What we store, and where

In your browser (local storage), on your device only: your language choice; that you dismissed the cookie notice; a login token, if you sign in (valid for 30 days); the code of an online room you are in, so you can rejoin; a progress log of up to 200 finished-round summaries (date, mode, topic, scores, fallacy names, judge persona); practice history of up to 20 attempts per topic (score, counts, word count); your latest personal lesson, if you made one; your study progress (which cards you know, your study streak, and best match times); the league code you entered, so you don't retype it; if you are a coach, the coach key and name of any league you created on this device; a random anonymous visitor ID used for usage counts (see below); and your choice if you opt out of those counts. Transcripts, claim logs, and held-back claims are not saved there. You can clear all of this in your browser settings.

On our servers (a database hosted through Vercel and provided by Neon), only if you use these features:

  • Account: email address, a bcrypt-hashed password (never the plain password), display name, optional bio (up to 500 characters) and profile picture (a small image your browser resizes), and sign-up date. If you are signed in, each finished round's summary (date, mode, topic, scores, judge persona, fallacy names) is also stored.
  • League code: if you enter one in Setup (or open your coach's join link), each finished round sends the code, the first name you typed in Setup, mode, topic, practice flag, judge persona, scores, and fallacy names. Anyone who has the code can see that league's data, including the student list, averages, and recent rounds.
  • Coaches who start a league: the league's name, its code, and a coach key that is stored only as a one-way hash (so it can't be shown again), plus any assignments the coach posts (title, topic, format, optional due date and note), which anyone with the league code can read. The coach key lets a coach post or delete assignments and delete a student's rounds from that league. CSV downloads are created in the coach's browser and are not stored by us.
  • Online Debate (requires an account): room code, mode, topic, the host's and guest's display names and account IDs, turn, timer and score state, the score log, and a text transcript of what each side said (up to 20,000 characters per turn). Only the host and the one person who joins with the room code can read it. We also record whether the room is Casual or Ranked and, for ranked rooms, the judge's result (winner, reason, and each player's rating change). Rooms are not currently deleted automatically.
  • Ranked rating (only for accounts that play ranked): your rating, games played, wins, losses, draws, highest rating, and whether you chose to show your name on the leaderboard (off by default); plus a record of each rated match (room code, both players' account IDs, the topic, the winner, and both ratings before and after). The leaderboard is visible to anyone who opens the app: it lists the top players' tier, rating, and number of games, and shows a name only for people who turned that option on (everyone else appears as "Anonymous debater"). Your opponent in a ranked match sees your display name, as in any online room. Ratings and match records are kept until we delete them; you can ask us to delete yours.

Anonymous usage counts. To see how the app is used, the app sends our server small events: that a page was opened (landing page or app), that a debate started or finished (mode, whether it was practice, minutes of speaking, number of turns), which features were used (for example turn review, Case Checker, Case Builder, AI judge, argument analysis, personal lesson, manual fact-check, upload, online room, league tools, study sets, Mock Trial case files and the objection log, ranked matches, high-accuracy listening), and how many AI calls were made in each category. Each event carries only the random visitor ID from your browser, a timestamp, and those few values. It never carries your name, email, speech, topics, transcripts, or IP address. Our code does not store IP addresses; the hosting provider's standard logs may (section 5). We honor Do Not Track and Global Privacy Control, and you can switch this off with the checkbox at the top of this policy; turning it off also deletes the visitor ID from your browser. These counts are deleted automatically after about 13 months (the cleanup runs as new events arrive).

Suggestion box. If you send a suggestion, we store its category, your message, and the email address if you choose to add one, until we delete it. Your browser's anonymous visitor ID is sent along only so we can limit spam; it is not saved with the suggestion. Don't put sensitive personal information in a suggestion.

We do not store audio, fact-check results, claim logs, or held-back claims on our servers. Our own code does not write request logs; our hosting provider keeps standard logs (see section 5).

Each of these handles data under its own terms and privacy policy, which we don't control — including how long it keeps data.
